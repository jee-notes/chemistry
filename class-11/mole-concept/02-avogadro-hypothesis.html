Avogadro's Hypothesis

The hypothesis that connected chemical laws with gas laws.

Gas Laws

The ideal gas equation

\[ PV=nRT \]

can be viewed as a combination of four independent relations.

  • Boyle's Law: \(V\propto \frac{1}{P}\)
  • Charles' Law: \(V\propto T\)
  • Avogadro's Hypothesis: \(V\propto n\)
  • The proportionality constant is the universal gas constant \(R\).

Unlike Boyle's and Charles' laws, the relation

\[ V\propto n \]

was not obtained directly from experiment. It was proposed by Amedeo Avogadro in 1811 as a hypothesis to explain the experimental observations available at that time.

The Problem Before Avogadro

By the beginning of the nineteenth century, chemists already knew that hydrogen and oxygen combine completely to form water.

\[ 1\text{ g Hydrogen} + 8\text{ g Oxygen} = 9\text{ g Water} \]

This showed that water is a combination product. During the reaction, hydrogen and oxygen do not merely mix; every gram of hydrogen and every gram of oxygen that reacts becomes part of water. Let us now examine this reaction using the atomic theory of matter.

According to the atomic theory, every substance is made up of tiny identical particles called atoms. Thus, a sample of hydrogen consists of hydrogen atoms, a sample of oxygen consists of oxygen atoms, and water consists of water particles. Since water is formed by complete combination, every hydrogen atom that reacts must combine with an oxygen atom to produce one water particle.

The only difference between hydrogen and oxygen particles is their mass. An oxygen particle is eight times heavier than a hydrogen particle. Therefore, a water particle formed by combining one hydrogen particle and one oxygen particle is heavier than both, and its mass is exactly equal to the sum of their masses.

This leads to an important conclusion. If one hydrogen particle combines with one oxygen particle to form one water particle, then 1 g of hydrogen, 8 g of oxygen and 9 g of water must each contain the same number of particles. The masses are different only because the particles themselves have different masses.

Everything appears perfectly consistent until Gay-Lussac's experiments on gases are considered. He discovered that hydrogen and oxygen combine in the simple volume ratio

\[ 2\text{ Vol Hydrogen} + 1\text{ Vol Oxygen} \rightarrow 2\text{ Vol Steam} \]

This created a serious difficulty.

If the particles present in hydrogen and oxygen gases were indeed individual atoms, then every hydrogen atom would require one oxygen atom to form water. Since equal numbers of hydrogen and oxygen atoms are needed, one would naturally expect equal volumes of the two gases to react.

\[ 2\text{ Vol Hydrogen} + 2\text{ Vol Oxygen} \rightarrow 2\text{ Vol Water Vapour} \]

However, experiments consistently showed that only one volume of oxygen was sufficient.

If every water particle requires one hydrogen atom and one oxygen atom, then how can one volume of oxygen supply enough oxygen atoms to combine completely with two volumes of hydrogen?

Avogadro's Hypothesis

When Gay-Lussac discovered that hydrogen and oxygen combine in the volume ratio

\[ 2\text{ Vol Hydrogen} + 1\text{ Vol Oxygen} \rightarrow 2\text{ Vol Steam} \]

scientists such as Avogadro found themselves in a genuine conceptual dilemma. The observed combining volumes could not be reconciled with the existing picture of atoms.

From the conclusions reached earlier, 1 g of hydrogen, 8 g of oxygen and 9 g of water were expected to contain the same number of particles, say n. Let the volume occupied by 8 g of oxygen be V. Then, according to Gay-Lussac's observations, the same number of hydrogen particles occupy a volume 2V, while the same number of water particles also occupy a volume 2V.

This immediately raises a fundamental question: What does the volume of a gas actually represent? It clearly cannot be determined simply by the masses of its particles. Oxygen particles are much heavier than hydrogen particles, yet they occupy only half the volume. Water particles are heavier still, yet they occupy the same volume as hydrogen.

To resolve this contradiction, Avogadro proposed that the volume of a gas is determined not by the masses of its particles, but by the number of gaseous particles present. In other words, at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of all gases contain equal numbers of gaseous particles.

This is Avogadro's famous hypothesis,

\[ V\propto n \]

which was later verified experimentally and became one of the fundamental relations of the ideal gas equation.

However, this immediately creates another puzzle. If equal volumes contain equal numbers of gaseous particles, then one volume of oxygen contains only half as many gaseous particles as two volumes of hydrogen. How then can complete combination occur when every hydrogen particle requires one oxygen particle?

Avogadro resolved this by proposing that the particles of elemental gases need not be individual atoms. Instead, they could themselves be combinations of atoms (molecules).

Thus, one oxygen gas particle could contain two oxygen atoms. During the reaction, each oxygen gas particle separates into two oxygen atoms, allowing one volume of oxygen gas to supply enough oxygen atoms for complete combination with two volumes of hydrogen gas.

In modern language, Avogadro's idea may be viewed as follows. If 1 g of hydrogen contains n hydrogen atoms, then 8 g of oxygen contains the same n oxygen atoms, but these are grouped into only n/2 oxygen gas particles. During the reaction, these n/2 oxygen gas particles separate into n oxygen atoms, which combine with the n hydrogen atoms to produce n water particles.

In modern notation,

\[ 2H_2+O_2\rightarrow2H_2O \]

Avogadro's hypothesis thus explained both Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes and the established laws of chemical combination, while also revealing that the particles of elemental gases are molecules rather than individual atoms.
